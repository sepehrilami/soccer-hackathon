The Lineup Puzzle

The Decision

Choosing a starting XI is arguably the most consequential and least formalized decision a football manager makes.

The Constraints

  • Exactly 11 players
  • Each assigned to a positional slot
  • Dictated by tactical formation
  • Optimize for individual quality and collective chemistry

The Current Practice

  • Intuition + scouting reports
  • Recent form & fitness
  • No systematic account of relational structure of team play

Key tension: The best 11 individuals may not be the best 11 as a team

Let me start with the fundamental problem. Selecting a starting lineup is one of the highest-stakes decisions in football. You need exactly 11 players, each fitting a specific positional slot, and you're trying to simultaneously optimize for individual talent AND how well those players work together as a unit. Currently, managers rely mostly on intuition. The key insight motivating this work is that the best 11 individuals may not be the best 11 as a team — there's a chemistry component that existing analytics completely miss.

The problem with current analytics is that they're fundamentally atomistic. xG, expected threat, VAEP — they all rate players independently. A player's xG doesn't depend on who's playing alongside them. Network science gives us the language to capture these relational dependencies. In a passing network, players are nodes and pass frequencies are weighted edges. The crucial insight is that a season-level passing network contains information about ALL possible pairwise combinations — even pairs that rarely played together. The network stores latent information about untested configurations.

Key Preprocessing

  • Extract all successful passes between teammates
  • Compute co-presence time Tij for each player pair
  • Normalize: passes per 90 min of shared time
  • Threshold: Tij ≥ 10 minutes

Co-presence normalization removes the confound of differential playing time — edge weights reflect passing affinity, not opportunity.

We used the IMPECT open data release covering all 306 matches of the 2023/24 Bundesliga. The dataset provides granular event-level data — every on-ball action with timestamps and coordinates. A critical preprocessing step is co-presence time normalization. Raw pass counts are confounded by differential playing time — a pair of regular starters will naturally accumulate more passes. We normalize by the total minutes each pair spent on the pitch together, converting to passes-per-90-minutes of shared time. This ensures edge weights reflect true passing affinity, not just opportunity. We threshold at 10 minutes of co-presence for stable rate estimates.

Our framework defines three complementary objectives. First, Individual Quality — the KPI score aggregates attacking output, creativity, ball progression, and defensive contributions, with positional normalization so we don't penalize center-backs for low xG. Second, Network Mobility — a composite of 12 centrality descriptors from the passing network: PageRank, betweenness, eigenvector centrality, clustering, and more. This captures each player's structural importance as a passing hub, broker, or connector. Third — and this is the novel part — Passing Cohesion. This operates at the GROUP level: given 11 selected players, we extract the induced subgraph and compute its mean edge weight. This directly measures how fluently the chosen group passes among themselves. The scalarized objective combines all three with user-controllable weights alpha, beta, and gamma.

Framework Overview

Solver: Greedy + Tabu Search

  • Greedy init: Fill 11 slots by largest marginal gain
  • Tabu search: 3,000 iterations of slot-preserving swaps
  • Tabu list prevents cycling (tenure = 50)
  • Returns best lineup encountered

Formation Constraints

  • 7 built-in templates: 4-3-3, 4-2-3-1, 4-4-2, 3-4-3, 3-5-2, 5-3-2, 4-1-4-1
  • Custom formations supported
  • Positional eligibility from season data

Here's the full pipeline. From the event data we construct the season-level passing network, compute player-level descriptors, and then solve the multi-objective optimization under formation constraints. The solver uses a two-stage approach: greedy initialization fills each formation slot with the best eligible player, then tabu search performs 3,000 iterations of slot-preserving swaps. The tabu mechanism prevents the search from cycling back to previously explored solutions. Despite the solution space being on the order of 10^6 to 10^8 feasible lineups, the solver completes in under 2 seconds on a standard laptop, making it fully interactive.

For external validation, we asked: do our optimized lineups perform well under a metric the optimizer never sees? We fit Ridge regressions predicting match-level expected goals from the starting XI, with team and opponent fixed effects. The results are encouraging. 13 of 18 teams have optimized lineups predicted to generate more xG than their typical match-day selection. On average, optimized XIs sit at the 58th percentile — above the median. The largest uplifts go to Darmstadt and Mainz — relegated and near-relegated teams with volatile selection — where the optimizer's XI outperforms EVERY lineup actually fielded. On the flip side, Stuttgart and Union Berlin show negative uplifts — their managers were already fielding near-optimal combinations. This discriminating behavior is exactly what we want: the framework helps struggling teams the most.

Key Insights

1. Chemistry Compensates for Talent

Stuttgart's surprise runner-up finish mirrors its highest cohesion score despite lower individual KPIs — strong internal chemistry lets teams punch above their weight.

2. Struggling Teams Gain Most

Darmstadt & Mainz — relegated and near-relegated — showed the largest xG uplifts (+0.27, +0.30). Volatile selection = biggest opportunity for optimization.

3. Weights Are a Real Dial

The Bayern sweep shows a clear phase transition between talent-led and chemistry-led regimes — not a cosmetic parameter, but a structural reconfiguration of the roster.

4. Stability in the Sweet Spot

The recommendation is stable for γ ∈ [0.2, 0.4] — small analyst disagreements about the chemistry emphasis don't destabilize the prescription.

Divergence between talent-heavy and chemistry-heavy XIs flags a disconnect in the squad.
Convergence means the squad's stars are also its connectors.

Let me highlight the four key insights from this work. First, chemistry compensates for talent — Stuttgart's story perfectly illustrates this. Second, struggling teams with volatile selection benefit most from principled optimization — that's where the biggest xG uplift margins are. Third, the weight vector is a genuine analytical dial — pushing it to extremes produces structurally different but interpretable lineups, not noise. Fourth, the recommendation is stable in a sweet spot around the default weights, meaning you don't need to precisely calibrate alpha, beta, gamma to get useful output. And there's a meta-insight: the gap between the talent-heavy and chemistry-heavy lineups itself is diagnostic — a large gap means the squad's best players aren't its best connected players, which is actionable information for squad building.

This slide details the 12 network descriptors we compute for each player. They span four analytical facets: Volume metrics like strength capture how much a player is involved in passing. Brokerage metrics — betweenness, flow betweenness, load, bridging — capture whether a player sits on critical pathways between teammates. Prestige metrics — PageRank and eigenvector centrality — reward players who receive passes from well-connected teammates. And topology metrics — clustering, local efficiency, constraint — describe the local structure around each player. A high-strength low-constraint player is a central distribution hub; a high-betweenness low-clustering player is a critical connector.

Cohesion Variants BACKUP

Arithmetic Mean (Default)

Mean edge weight of induced subgraph. Captures passing volume

COH(S) = mean(Wij) for all pairs in S

Geometric Mean

Amplifies penalty on any single weak link. Sensitive to weakest pair

COH(S) = exp(mean(log Wij))

Algebraic Connectivity

Second-smallest Laplacian eigenvalue. Captures robustness to disconnection

COH(S) = λ2(LS)

Shannon Entropy

Normalized entropy of edge weight distribution. Captures passing balance

COH(S) = H(S) / Hmax ∈ [0, 1]
💡

Entropy rewards balance: teams whose passing is simultaneously structured and difficult to predict tend to outperform more centralized opponents (Martínez-Cagigal et al., Neuman & Cohen).

BACKUP SLIDE
The framework implements four cohesion variants capturing different facets of subgraph structure. The arithmetic mean is the default — pure passing volume. The geometric mean penalizes any single weak pair more heavily. Algebraic connectivity — the Fiedler value — measures robustness to disconnection and has been linked to team performance in prior literature. Shannon entropy captures passing BALANCE rather than volume — it rewards lineups where no single pair dominates the passing structure. This is motivated by recent work showing that teams with balanced, unpredictable passing distributions outperform centralized opponents.

Limitations & Future Work BACKUP

Current Limitations

  • Season-level only: no opponent-specific tactics, short-term form, injury/fitness
  • Stationarity assumption: passing relationships treated as fixed across the season
  • Passes only: misses off-ball movement, pressing intensity, spatial positioning
  • Correlational validation: xG uplifts fall within residual noise; causal claims need interventional evidence

Extensions

  • Opponent-conditioned: optimize against specific defensive structures
  • Temporal networks: rolling-window or time-varying models for mid-season shifts
  • Learned weights: α, β, γ from match outcomes rather than user-set
  • Cross-league: IMPECT pipeline generalizes to any league with event data
  • Cohesion comparison: which variant best predicts league points?
  • Managerial alignment: does overlap with optimal XI predict performance?
BACKUP SLIDE
Several limitations warrant discussion. The framework operates at the season level — it doesn't account for opponent-specific tactics, injuries, or short-term form. It assumes passing relationships are stationary across the season, which isn't always true as tactical systems evolve. And it only looks at passes, missing off-ball movement and pressing. The xG validation is correlational, not causal. For future work, opponent-conditioned optimization, temporal networks with rolling windows, learning the weights from match outcomes, and cross-league generalization are all natural extensions. We'd also like to systematically compare the four cohesion variants and test whether managerial alignment with the optimal XI predicts in-season performance.

KPI Scoring Details BACKUP

Six Facets of Player Quality

Attacking: Goals, shot-based xG, post-shot xG

Creative: xA, assists, packing-type passes

Progression: Bypassed opponents & defenders

Passing: Successful passes, expected passes

Defensive: Ball wins (including teammates)

Ball Security (neg): Ball losses, unsuccessful passes

Key Design Choices

Positional Normalization

Z-scores computed within {GK, DEF, MID, ATT} groups — center-backs aren't penalized for low attacking output vs. strikers

Consistency Penalty

KPI(i) = Σ wk(z(μ) − η · z(σ)) — penalizes volatile performers with high match-to-match variance

Reliability Weighting

ri = 0.25 + 0.75 · √(mi/max mj) — scales positive scores by playing time; negative scores kept intact to avoid spurious boosting

BACKUP SLIDE
The KPI score aggregates six facets of player quality. The sign of ball-security metrics is flipped so higher always means better. Three important design choices: First, positional normalization — we z-score within positional groups so center-backs aren't compared against strikers on xG. Second, a consistency penalty controlled by eta penalizes players with high match-to-match variance — we want reliable performers. Third, reliability weighting scales scores by playing time, but only for POSITIVE scores — discounting a negative score would make a bad low-minute player look better, which is a subtle but important asymmetry.

For those interested in the regression methodology — we fit two Ridge specifications. M1 includes player dummies and team fixed effects only. M2 adds opponent fixed effects. This is crucial because it absorbs the variability in defensive difficulty — generating 1.5 xG against Leverkusen is not the same as against a relegation team. M1 actually performs worse than the naive team-mean baseline — match-level xG is dominated by opponent variability that swamps the player signal. M2 is the first specification to beat the baseline, with R-squared of 0.192. The Ridge penalty of lambda=30 indicates substantial regularization is needed given the wide design matrix relative to the 612 observations. All predictions are made against a baseline opponent to ensure fair apples-to-apples comparisons.
